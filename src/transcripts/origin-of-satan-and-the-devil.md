# The Origin of Satan and the Devil

Most people assume that the Bible has a lot to say about the Devil and Satan, that the Scriptures clearly describe a supernatural being who opposes God. In fact, this is such a commonly held belief that many readers never stop to ask a very important question. Is the Satan you know, with red horns and a pitchfork, actually what the Old Testament teaches? In this video we are going to explore the origins of the Devil and Satan by carefully looking at the way that they're described throughout the scripture, their origins in history, and the writings that shaped the common beliefs today.

When most people think about Satan, they imagine a rebellious angel, a ruler of demons, and a cosmic enemy of God. Yet when we turn to the Old Testament, that picture is surprisingly absent. In the Hebrew Scriptures, the word *satan* simply means adversary. It's not inherently a proper name. It's a description of a role.

Often that adversary is human. Other times it's an angel acting on God's behalf. For example, in the book of Job, "the satan" appears among God's servants. He's not portrayed as God's rival, nor as a rogue being operating independently. He only acts with divine permission.

Even more striking is what we don't find. There's no account of a fallen angel rebellion. There's no reference to a kingdom of demons ruled by the Devil. There's no supernatural force struggling against God for control of the universe.

Instead, the Old Testament consistently presents God as the sole source of power. Even troubling spirits are shown to be under divine authority. Judges chapter 9, verse 23 says that:

> "God stirred up animosity between Abimelech and the citizens of Shechem."

In 1 Samuel 16, verse 14, we read that:

> "A distressing spirit from the Lord troubled Saul."

The emphasis is always the same: God remains sovereign.

This very same pattern appears when we examine demons. Many readers assume that demons are everywhere in the Scripture. Yet in the Hebrew Old Testament, demons are rare and consistently associated with idolatry. Leviticus chapter 17 warns Israel not to sacrifice to demons. Deuteronomy chapter 32 says that Israel sacrificed to demons, not to God; to gods whom they knew not. In other words, demons aren't described as independent supernatural beings. They are another term for false gods, objects of misguided worship.

So, if the Old Testament does not clearly describe a supernatural Devil, then where did that common understanding come from? To answer that, we need to look at the centuries between the Old and the New Testaments. During that time, Judaism underwent enormous cultural change. The Jewish people lived under Persian rule, then Greek rule, and eventually Roman rule. Languages shifted from Hebrew to Aramaic and to Greek, leading to different translations of Scripture — most notably the Septuagint. Translations, as you might know, do not merely transfer words. They often reflect interpretation. And in the Septuagint, we begin to notice subtle but important shifts.

Passages that originally described pestilence or wilderness imagery begin to take on new language. In the Septuagint version, Psalm 90, verse 6, speaks of a "demon at midday." Isaiah passages start to sound far more supernatural in the Greek than they do in the Hebrew. What were once descriptions of wilderness creatures now appear as demons. And why the change? Well because beliefs within the community were evolving.

During this same period, Jewish writers produced numerous non-biblical texts. Books such as Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, and Wisdom present a far more developed supernatural world. In those writings, we encounter fallen angels, rebellious spirits, and Satan portrayed as a supernatural leader of evil forces.

For example, Wisdom 2, verse 24, famously states:

> "Through the envy of the devil came death into the world."

Tobit centers around a demon named Asmodeus. These portrayals are striking — not because they repeat Old Testament teaching, but because they expand on it.

This represented a major theological development, because these ideas are not drawn directly from the Hebrew Old Testament. They emerge later, during the period of cultural and intellectual change.

By the time we reach the New Testament, this evolved worldview was already present. Demons and Satanic language had become familiar concepts within the culture. That's why, while the Old Testament is largely silent on a supernatural Devil, later writings seem to speak of one frequently.

What this means is that the concept of a supernatural Devil didn't suddenly appear fully formed. It developed over time through translation, cultural interaction, and expanding interpretation. The Old Testament though, presents a remarkably consistent message: God alone is sovereign. God alone is the ultimate source of power. And to properly recognize this, reshapes how we understand evil, responsibility, and human accountability.
