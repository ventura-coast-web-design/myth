# Lucifer

Many people assume that Lucifer is the original name of a powerful supernatural being often identified with Satan. But is that actually what the Bible teaches? Well to answer that question, we need to look carefully at where the word appears in the scripture and what the surrounding context says.

Surprisingly the word Lucifer only appears once in the entire Bible and only in certain translations. In the King James Version, we read in Isaiah chapter 14:

> "How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning! How art thou cut down to the ground, which didst weaken the nations!"

At first glance this language sounds dramatic, someone falling from heaven and being brought down to judgment. Because of this imagery many readers have assumed that Isaiah must be describing the fall of a rogue angel turned supernatural devil. But before drawing that conclusion we should ask an important question. Who is Isaiah actually talking about? And to answer that, we need to take a step back and look at the context of Isaiah's prophecy.

Beginning in Isaiah chapter 13, the prophet records a series of messages against various nations that had oppressed Israel. Those nations included Babylon, Moab, Syria, Egypt, and others. Each prophecy describes how God would hold those nations accountable for their cruelty and their arrogance. When we arrive at Isaiah chapter 14, the passage itself clearly tells us who the message is being directed toward. In verse 4 Isaiah writes:

> "Take up this proverb against the king of Babylon…"

So, the subject of Isaiah chapter 14 is the king of Babylon.

Babylon was one of the most powerful empires that the world had ever seen. Under kings like Nebuchadnezzar, its influence stretched across the ancient Near East, from the Fertile Crescent all the way toward Egypt. Babylon conquered nations, destroyed cities, and eventually invaded Jerusalem itself, burning the temple and carrying the people of Israel into captivity. To many, Babylon must have seemed unstoppable, but Isaiah's prophecy declared that this powerful kingdom wouldn't last forever. In fact, the chapter describes how the nations that Babylon had once oppressed would eventually rejoice when its power collapsed. Isaiah writes that the earth would finally be at rest and the nations would say:

> "You too have become as weak as we, you have become like us."

This mighty ruler who once terrified the world would be brought low. Isaiah even describes people looking at this fallen king and saying:

> "Is this the man who made the earth tremble, and who shook kingdoms?"

Now notice that phrase carefully, "is this the man." So, the prophecy is speaking about a human ruler, not some supernatural being.

So where then does this word Lucifer come from? Well, the term actually comes from the Latin Vulgate, which is a Latin translation of the Bible which was produced in the 4th century and was primarily used for about a thousand years. Which explains its impact on the King James Version. In that translation the Hebrew word *helel* was rendered as Lucifer and it simply means a light bearer or morning star. The original Hebrew phrase essentially means the same thing, shining one, or morning star. So because of this, most modern Bible translations actually render the verse differently. For example, instead of saying Lucifer, the ESV translates it, "O Day star, son of dawn." The New American Standard Bible translates it, "you star of the morning, son of the dawn."

So, Isaiah is using poetic imagery. In the ancient world the morning star, often associated with the planet Venus, appears very bright in the sky just before sunrise. But as the sun rises that bright star quickly fades and disappears and that imagery perfectly describes what happened to Babylon. The kingdom grows quickly and it's shown with incredible power and wealth, but just as quickly it disappeared from its place of glory when it was conquered by the Persian Empire.

So, Isaiah's prophecy was describing the rise and sudden fall of a proud earthly ruler. And this theme of pride, it's something we see clearly illustrated in the life of Nebuchadnezzar himself. In the book of Daniel, the king boasts about the greatness of the kingdom he built only to quickly be humbled by God. So, the message of Isaiah 14 isn't about a supernatural being falling from heaven, instead it's a powerful poetic description of how human pride leads to downfall. This ruler who once exalted himself above all the nations would ultimately be brought down to the grave.

This passage also reminds us of something very important when reading through the Bible and that's that context matters. When we read verses in isolation, it's easy to misunderstand what the prophets were actually saying, but when we read these surrounding chapters and pay attention to who actually is being addressed, the meaning becomes much clearer. Isaiah wasn't revealing the origin of a supernatural devil named Lucifer, he was warning that even the most powerful kingdoms and rulers are subject to God's judgment. Because no matter how bright a kingdom might shine for a moment, pride and oppression will eventually bring it crashing down.
